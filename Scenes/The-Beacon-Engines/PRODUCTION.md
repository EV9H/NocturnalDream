## Direction in two lines

The Ming Great Wall at blue dawn, seen by a walker climbing it, and on the summit ahead a beacon tower that never stopped growing: a 400-metre engine of brick, timber bracket sets, bronze gears and a water wheel. It lights its signal, and the engines on the far ridges answer one after another to the horizon.

## Treatment (for owner approval)

**Logline.** Climbing the Great Wall at dawn, you reach the moment the beacon tower on the next summit, grown into a mountain-sized water engine, lights the signal, and the whole Wall answers.

**First frame.** Eye height on the walkway at Jinshanling, at the foot of a long flight of worn steps, 24mm, level. Crenellated outer parapet on the left, low inner wall on the right, a porter with firewood on a shoulder pole climbing fifteen metres ahead, back to us. A two-storey watchtower halfway up. On the summit about a kilometre away, the beacon engine: brick base, eight timber storeys, a water wheel turning in its base, a bronze ring crown entering thin cloud. To the right the Wall runs on over lower ridges, each with a smaller engine. Nothing burns yet except two small lanterns.

**The turn.** The viewer first sees "the Great Wall", then reads the tower as impossible from its size against the watchtower and the tiny guards on its galleries. The second understanding comes from the machinery: the flume feeding the wheel, the spill down the mountainside, the bare logged slope, the soot on the top eaves. It is a working machine that burns timber and runs on water.

**The held moment.** 18–30 s. The ring turns, fire catches in the bowl and flashes through the turning panels, smoke climbs into the cloud. Then, on the right, the next engine flashes, then the next, smaller and later, the last a point in the haze. Six seconds of hold while the gongs arrive from further and further away.

**World rules and evidence (alternate history, labelled).**
- *Real:* Ming frontier towers (烟墩 / 墩台) on high points along and near the Wall relayed alarms: smoke by day, fire by night, and in the Ming, gun or cannon reports (烽炮) whose count rose with the size of the enemy force. Popular sources describe a code of one fire and one shot for up to about a hundred raiders, rising to several for over a thousand, and cite a regulation of 成化二年 (1466). Popular sources give tower spacing of about 2.5–5 km. Towers also used flags by day and lanterns by night. *Not verified against a primary text (《明实录》 / 《大明会典》). Do not state the 1466 date or the exact code in the post until checked.*
- *Real precedent for the machinery:* water-powered bellows (水排, Eastern Han, attributed to 杜诗) and timber bracket sets (斗拱).
- *Fork:* the beacon towers kept being enlarged, and Ming engineers put water and timber to work. A flume carries spring water along the ridge to a wheel in each tower's base. The wheel drives bellows for the fire and, through bronze gears on one timber shaft, turns a ring of bronze shutter panels around the fire bowl, so the flame flashes in a countable rhythm visible across ridges.
- *Evidence:* flume on trestles, white spill down the slope, wet dark brick, bronze gears with patina, soot-black top eaves, stumps on a logged slope, stacked firewood on the Wall, a porter carrying more, guards on the galleries.
- *Cost:* the mountains are stripped of timber to feed the fires, and the water is spent down the slope.
- *Era anchor:* Ming only. Grey brick, crenellations, grey tile eaves, cotton padded clothing. No later or fantastical technology.

**Look.** Megastructure Sublime (chapter 03 card 4): one dominant structure, atmospheric depth by ridge, bronze, timber and grey brick, one hot accent. Borrowed device: handheld breath from Golden Naturalism for the POV. The accent is earned: the frame stays cold blue until the signal fire, which is the colour event.

**Sound.** Listener on the Wall's walkway, walking. Bed: cold wind across the parapet. Mid: water through the wheel and timber groan, distant. Close: footsteps on brick, breath. Hero: the bronze bell, then the ring's grinding turn and the fire's roar. Then the chain of answering gongs, each fainter and later. Silence falls under the wind for the last two seconds. No music, no speech. Generated audio is a draft; plan to rebuild in post.

**Audience (hypotheses).**
- *Viewer:* anyone in China who has climbed or seen the Wall. Generation reach: all ages.
- *Waste-of-time test:* a feeling (awe at scale, then the signal crossing the mountains) and a "wait, what?" (how it works) worth a rewatch.
- *Likely comments:* "原来烽火台是这样", "这是金山岭吧", "古人要是真造出来了". Fact-checkers: "明代是放炮的" — credit the real history in the description to invite them.
- *Sent to:* someone who climbed the Wall with them, with "我们爬的时候怎么没有这个".
- *Retell:* "长城的烽火台长成了山那么大的机器，一点火整条长城都亮了。"
- *STEPPS:* Emotion (awe), Social Currency (a real history fork they can explain), Public (a signature structure for a series).

**Hardest problem and the smallest test.**
1. The engine must be huge yet believable, and the Ming Wall details right. Test: the keyframe batch, checked against real Jinshanling photos for steps, parapet, loopholes and watchtower windows.
2. A 30 s continuous take may drift (engine shape, far-engine order, the porter). Test: the one free run, reviewed at 0.5 s intervals. Fallback edit: cut to the hook plus the 18–30 s payoff.

### Changes from the handoff beats (my decisions, flagged for approval)
- **No arrival at the engine's foot.** A walker covers about 15–20 m of steps in 14 s, not a kilometre, and a 400 m engine at its foot would need a near-vertical tilt that invites drift. The walker stops on a landing and lifts the gaze about ten degrees instead. The water wheel, bell and steam read at that distance on a wide, simple structure.
- **The far engines sit to the right of the near one in the same frame**, so the payoff needs no pan or head turn.
- **Fire, not smoke, carries the chain** (smoke for day, fire for night is the real rule; blue dawn sits between). Smoke rises from the near engine as evidence.


### Proposed change after the keyframe tests (2026-11-19, needs owner approval)
- **Weather and season:** early autumn, the morning after rain. A low still cloud deck, a sea of cloud in the valleys, a wet Wall. This replaces clear late-autumn blue dawn with valley mist. Reason: it matches when Jinshanling's real 云海 forms. It keeps orange out of the sky until the fire, and it hides the engine's base and crown, which carries the scale (REVIEW.md, v05b).
- **Payoff:** each signal fire lights the underside of the cloud deck orange above its engine, so the answering chain reads across the sky as well as on the ridges.
- **Siting (owner note, 2026-11-19):**
  - Each engine stands apart from the Wall, on its own summit on the inner side, as real beacon towers stood apart from it.
  - A spur wall joins it to the Wall from a watchtower's inner gate. Its walled yard holds fuel, a cistern and a guard hut.
  - Its water comes from inland. Its gate faces the Wall.
  - Nobody lives in it: soldiers live in the watchtowers, where real fires were never lit.
  - See REVIEW.md, v06.
- **Crown with five fire bowls (proposal):** the number lit could carry the message, after the real code of up to five fires.
- **Video prompt v01** still describes blue dawn, clear air and low mist. It needs a v02 once a keyframe is chosen.

## Director's statement v2 (proposed 2026-11-19, needs owner approval)

**The story in one line.** One message crosses a thousand li of mountains in the time it takes to say it, and it is fed by a man with a shoulder pole who will never see where it goes.

**What it is about.** Not the machine. The machine is the means. The film is about the distance between the people who keep the fire and the places the fire reaches. The awe is the scale and the speed. The quiet feeling underneath is that every flame on the horizon was carried up a mountain on someone's back, and the hillsides are bare because of it. That answers the depth test ("what is being lost?"): the forest, and the porter's day, burned to send one sentence.

**Why the earlier frames fail as cinema** (my judgment):
- They are world-building plates, not photographs. Every element is shown at once, evenly weighted, so the frame explains instead of making you look.
- The composition is the tourist postcard of the Great Wall: centred on the walkway, steps leading to a watchtower. The engine is pasted on the right like a sticker.
- The porter walks away from the engine, toward nothing. He and the engine have no relationship in the frame.
- The light has no event. The overcast is right for waiting, but nothing in the frame is about to happen.

**The shot.** One continuous 30 s take. The camera is a companion walking three or four metres behind the porter at his pace, not his eyes, so his body gives scale and the camera never needs feet.
- **First frame (hook).** On the Wall, just before the watchtower's inner gate, 35–40 mm at eye height. The porter is lower left, his load wide in the frame. The Wall runs diagonally up to the watchtower. Above and behind the watchtower, the engine's dark crown and upper storeys push into the cloud deck, upper right third. One second is enough to read the Great Wall, and a thing above it that cannot be real. The rest of the engine is hidden by the watchtower and the ridge.
- **The turn.** He passes through the gate onto the spur, and we follow. The spur opens to the right, and the whole engine is revealed across the saddle: yard, wheel, flume, crown. The reveal comes through movement, the way a person would actually discover it.
- **The event.** A bell. He stops and sets his load down. The crown turns, and fire catches in two of the five bowls. The underside of the cloud deck blooms orange above the engine.
- **The payoff (the one image).** The camera pans right with his head, following the light. Along the horizon the cloud deck lights in patches, one engine after another, a fuse burning under the sky along the Wall. The gongs arrive late, because sound travels slower than light: about three seconds per kilometre.
- **The end.** He picks up the pole again. The last frame rhymes with the first: his back, the load, the Wall, the crown now burning. It loops.

**One-image test (hypothesis):** "a line of fire running under the clouds along the Great Wall."

**Look.** Megastructure Sublime, carried by restraint:
- Overcast deck, Portra-like, low contrast.
- The only warm colour is fire, saved until 18 s.
- 35–40 mm, handheld with breath, deep but not infinite focus.
- The porter sharp, the far chain soft.

**Sound.**
- Wind and his breathing under load, the pole creaking, footsteps on wet brick.
- The bell. Two seconds of near silence. The roar of fire.
- Gongs from further and further away, each later and thinner.
- No music.

### Fact check of statement v2 (2026-11-19, after owner asked whether this is how it really worked)
- **Supported by sources** ([北京日报 2026](https://news.bjd.com.cn/2026/08/07/11904411.shtml), [网易 烽火台](https://m.163.com/dy/article/FDFL5L390541AGHL.html)):
  - Beacon towers mostly stand apart from the Wall, on both its inner and outer sides.
  - The hollow watchtowers held soldiers, grain and gunpowder, so fires were not lit on them.
  - Smoke and fire were usually made in several fire pits beside the beacon tower, and the number carried the message.
  - Smoke by day, fire by night, plus gun reports (号炮) in the Ming. The popular sources cite a 成化二年 code of "one fire, one gun" for small raids, up to "five fires, five guns" for over a thousand. Primary text not checked.
  - Some beacon towers had a small walled enclosure (坞城).
- **Corrections to my plan:**
  - The answering sound should be **号炮 (gun reports)**, not gongs, and their count should match the fires: two fires, two reports. I am also dropping the bell, which has no source.
  - **A real beacon tower had its own small garrison (墩军) living at it**, in or beside the enclosure (general knowledge, unverified). So "nobody lives in the engine" is wrong. Soldiers live in its yard and the hut, and the porter brings supplies to them.
- **Invented (alternate history, label as such):**
  - The spur wall linking the Wall to the beacon. Unverified whether any existed; treat as invented.
  - The water engine, the turning crown and the five bowls.
  - Porters carrying fuel along the spur. The fuel store is real in spirit; who carried it is not sourced.
- **Gongs → guns in the sound plan.** The delay rule still holds: light arrives first, and the reports about three seconds per kilometre later.

### Clarification: where the fire comes from (2026-11-19)
- **People carry fuel, not fire.** The porter brings firewood up because a signal fire burns a great deal of wood. That is the cost.
- **The fire is kept in the engine.** The garrison keeps an ember fire (火种) burning day and night in a covered iron brazier inside the engine's lowest gallery. The two small lanterns seen in the keyframes are its glow.
- **How the crown lights (invented):** the water wheel drives bellows (水排, a real Eastern Han invention) that blow the ember fire up an iron flue into the crown, and the chosen bowls catch.
- **Story line, revised to avoid the ambiguity:** "火是机关自己点的，可烧的每一根柴，都是人挑上山的。"
